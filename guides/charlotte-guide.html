Road Trip Stop — Charlotte, NC

A banking-boomtown skyline sitting on top of a leveled Black business district, a legendary punk club nobody puts on a postcard, and a former Ford/missile plant now doing duty as the city's closest thing to a maker district.

✦ Pop-Culture Connections

Films Shot Here

  • Logan Lucky (2017) — dir. Steven Soderbergh. Charlotte Motor Speedway — Much of the heist-film photography was shot at the speedway during and around race weekends; production also used Atlanta Motor Speedway repainted to match Charlotte's then-yellow walls (a former Sprint sponsorship color). The speedway itself is a working NASCAR track with a public souvenir shop and a Speedway Club restaurant.

Notable People Born / Raised Here

  • Carson McCullers — Novelist associated with the Southern Gothic tradition (The Heart Is a Lonely Hunter); lived in Charlotte for a period while writing.
  • Skeet Ulrich (b. 1970) — Raised in the Charlotte area; played Billy Loomis in Scream (1996), one of the defining slasher performances of the '90s horror revival.
  • DaBaby — Charlotte-raised rapper; much of his early mixtape-era mythology and video geography is tied to the city.
  • Anthony Hamilton — Charlotte-born R&B singer.
  • Dorothy Counts — In 1957, at 15, photographed being jeered and spat on while integrating Harry Harding High School — one of the era's most widely circulated civil rights images. A mural of her now stands at Camp North End, near the Brooklyn/Second Ward site.
  • Harvey B. Gantt — Not born in Charlotte (Charleston, SC) but the city's first Black mayor (1983) and the first Black student admitted to Clemson; the Gantt Center for African-American Arts + Culture, opened 2009 near the former Brooklyn site, carries his name.

Brooklyn / Second Ward — The Green & Gantt Center ◆ Thread Anchor
PoliticalLiterary
Brooklyn was Charlotte's Black business, church, and school district — home to the city's first Black school, its only Black high school until 1969, an AME Zion publishing house, and a full commercial strip — razed almost entirely under 1960s urban renewal. The Green park and the Harvey B. Gantt Center for African-American Arts + Culture (2009) now sit on and near the site; Brooklyn Village Ave (renamed from Stonewall St, 2022) marks the ongoing redevelopment reckoning.
