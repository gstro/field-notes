Road Trip Stop — Washington, DC

The federal capital and, structurally, its own case study in disenfranchisement -- taxation without representation on the license plates, U Street's "Black Broadway" gutted by the 1968 riots and gentrified since, and a hardcore-punk DIY ethic (Minor Threat, Fugazi, Bad Brains, Dischord Records, straight edge) as consequential as any city's. Six nights here, the longest stop of Trip 2.

✦ Pop-Culture Connections

Films Shot Here

  • The Exorcist (1973) — the climactic death scene was shot on the steep Georgetown stairway now called the Exorcist Steps (Prospect St & Canal Rd NW), a designated DC landmark since 2015. Georgetown University's Healy Hall and Dahlgren Chapel also appear.
  • All the President's Men (1976) — DC shooting began May 1975; the Washington Post newsroom was meticulously recreated on a Burbank soundstage, but the story runs directly through the real Watergate complex in Foggy Bottom.
  • St. Elmo's Fire (1985) — set at Georgetown University, but the school refused filming permission over the script's content, so campus scenes were shot instead at the University of Maryland.
  • Correction: House of Cards, the most-cited "DC show" of the streaming era, was shot almost entirely in Baltimore, not DC — the District's restrictive permitting pushes most current production north.

Notable People Born Here

  • Duke Ellington (b. 1899) — jazz composer/bandleader, came up on U Street; the Duke Ellington School of the Arts is named for him.
  • Marvin Gaye (b. 1939) — soul icon, born at Freedmen's Hospital, raised in DC public housing before Motown.
  • Ian MacKaye (b. 1962) — co-founder of Dischord Records (1980), frontman of Minor Threat and Fugazi; his lyric "Straight Edge" gave a whole hardcore subculture its name.
  • Henry Rollins (b. 1961) — fronted DC's State of Alert before joining Black Flag in 1981.
  • Eleanor Holmes Norton (b. 1937) — SNCC/Freedom Summer organizer, first woman to chair the EEOC, DC's non-voting House delegate since 1991, founding member of the Congressional Progressive Caucus.
  • Bad Brains (formed DC, 1976) — Dr. Know, Darryl Jenifer, H.R. and Earl Hudson turned from jazz fusion to some of the fastest, most technically accomplished hardcore ever recorded, before relocating to NYC around 1980.

Note: visit window is late May 2026 — well after peak cherry blossom season (typically late March–early April). The Tidal Basin is still worth a stroll; the blossoms themselves will be gone.

⚠ Current Status Note:
The Potter's House (Adams Morgan) — Adams Morgan's oldest locally owned bookstore-cafe, a pay-what-you-can nonprofit space since 1960 with deep roots in DC's peace and civil-rights organizing — is currently closed "for the foreseeable future." Its online bookstore remains active. This was the guide's strongest lead on a true DC infoshop-style radical bookstore/cafe, and its closure is noted here rather than papered over.
⚠ Correction:
Red Emma's, the worker-cooperative anarchist bookstore/cafe often mentally filed under "DC," is actually in Baltimore, MD (3128 Greenmount Ave) — excluded from this guide's DC-proper picks for that reason.
⚠ Financial distress:
Compass Coffee, DC's best-known local roaster chain, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in January 2026. Store footprint may be actively shrinking — call ahead before planning around a specific location.
⚠ Relocating:
Crooked Beat Records, one of DC's longest-running vinyl shops, was mid-relocation from Adams Morgan to Del Ray in Alexandria, VA as of this guide's writing — still Metro-reachable but no longer a DC-proper stop.
Honest gap — Bookstores: the mandatory anarchist/radical/infoshop search found no active DC-proper worker-owned radical bookstore. The strongest historical candidate (The Potter's House) is closed; the closest true infoshop-model space (Red Emma's) is in Baltimore. DC's actual radical-left infrastructure runs instead through explicitly political community hubs that are commercial rather than cooperative — Busboys and Poets, Sankofa, Solid State Books — plus organizing groups like DSA DC with no public storefront. A federal capital with enormous nonprofit/advocacy-org density produces a different radical-bookselling ecosystem than a college town or port city — structurally different, not weaker.
Honest gap — DIY Electronics: Category 10 carries four picks, not five, and two of the four (Rhizome's general-craft focus, Chuck Levin's location in Wheaton, MD) are adjacent rather than dead-center matches. DC has an excellent hackerspace (HacDC) and free public fab labs through the library system, but no dedicated synth shop or boutique pedal manufacturer on par with Keeley Electronics (Oklahoma City) or Squarewave Sound (Salt Lake City). Padding to five would misrepresent the city.

Next Steps

Deeper Dive The U Street "Black Broadway" walk deserves a standalone Field Notes entry of its own — Duke Ellington's and Marvin Gaye's neighborhood, gutted by the 1968 riots, slow-rebuilt over decades, now under a second wave of gentrification pressure. Ben's Chili Bowl, Busboys and Poets, and the 9:30 Club's current site all sit on the same few blocks.
Political / Historical Thread DC extends the "dispossession-and-precedence" thread first anchored at Oklahoma City's Deep Deuce and continued at Richmond's Jackson Ward/St. Luke Penny Savings Bank the leg before this one — U Street is DC's version of the same pattern: a self-sufficient Black business district disinvested, then reinvested-and-displaced.
Cross-City Link Dischord Records' DIY, no-lawyers, five-dollar-show model is the direct ancestor of the all-ages ethic this guide series keeps finding in smaller cities (Boise's Shredder, OKC's The Sanctuary) — worth tracing forward to see how the model holds up in Philadelphia, the next stop.
